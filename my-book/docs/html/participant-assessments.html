Special Education Professional Learning: Practical LLM Use

Participant Pre- and Post-Assessment

This assessment measures practical judgment when using large language models (LLMs) in special education planning, instruction, related services, and progress monitoring. It is not a test of prompt-engineering terminology or a measure of educator competence.

Administration at a glance

FormWhen to administerTimeMaximum score
Pre-assessmentBefore Session 120–25 minutes14
Post-assessmentAfter the Session 10 capstone20–25 minutes14

The post-assessment is not a Session 6 exit test. See the Course Map and Assessment Plan for the full schedule and how to interpret results alongside the separate AI-literacy assessment and capstone.

Both forms use parallel tasks so participants can demonstrate growth without memorizing identical answers.

Participant directions

  • Use fictional information only. Do not enter student records into an AI tool for this assessment.
  • You may complete the assessment with an approved LLM or describe what you would ask the model to do.
  • Answer independently before discussing with peers.
  • Explain your reasoning. A defensible decision matters more than matching a preferred phrase.
  • If a question lacks enough information, state what is missing rather than guessing.
  • Each item is scored from 0 to 2. Maximum: 14 points per form.

Facilitator answer notes

Accept equivalent answers when the reasoning is sound. Do not require participants to use identical wording.

Pre-assessment

  1. Prompt boundaries: A strong response asks for one bounded planning task, supplies only relevant approved context, prohibits invented facts and diagnosis, requests unknowns and questions, specifies an accessible output, and stops before recommending goals or services.
  2. Facts and unknowns: Accept facts explicitly stated in the selected case. A broad claim of independence is not established unless the case supplies task-specific evidence across relevant conditions. Ask what tasks, settings, supports, and opportunities have actually been observed; include the learner’s perspective.
  3. Goal measurement: Missing elements include the specific behavior/task, conditions, opportunities, operational definition, independence/prompt levels, data method, baseline, and individualized criterion.
  4. Data interpretation: No. Report 7/10 independently and 9/10 with verbal prompts separately. Confirm the same steps, valid opportunities, prompt definitions, materials, and conditions.
  5. Plateau analysis: Consider insufficient observations, task variability, inconsistent materials, prompt changes, environment, fatigue, attendance, opportunity count, instructional fidelity, and measurement reliability.
  6. Email triage: State that the message says “before the lesson tomorrow” and that the exact clock time is not stated. Urgency depends on lesson timing and preparation impact. Verify the original thread and local schedule; do not invent a time.
  7. Privacy and responsibility: Use approved tools and minimum necessary information. Keep evaluation, eligibility, placement, services, IEP decisions, and professional instructional judgment with qualified professionals and the team.

Post-assessment

  1. Prompt boundaries: The model may summarize patterns, check calculations, identify missing data, and offer competing explanations. It must not decide whether an IEP goal changes. Request a verified description and stop before a recommendation is adopted.
  2. Facts and unknowns: Accept facts explicitly stated in the selected case. Do not assume one support, communication mode, or strategy fits every activity. Ask the learner about access, effectiveness, effort, preferences, and task demands; consult qualified professionals and the team.
  3. Goal measurement: Specify the task, learner-relevant conditions and supports, number of valid opportunities, observable completion criteria, prompt or assistance definitions where relevant, measurement method, and baseline.
  4. Data interpretation: Report 6/8 independently and 8/8 with a visual prompt separately. Verify what the prompt was, whether steps and conditions were equivalent, and whether opportunities were valid.
  5. Plateau analysis: Check whether data points are comparable, inspect the different materials, document opportunity counts, review prompt and accommodation consistency, check instruction and environment, and gather learner feedback.
  6. Email triage: The due date is not stated. Urgency is unclear until task impact and time sensitivity are known. Ask the sender or check context; do not rank by sender title alone.
  7. Privacy and responsibility: Use district-approved tools, minimum necessary approved information, and privacy/retention safeguards. Do not delegate evaluation, eligibility, placement, services, IEP decisions, or professional instructional judgment.

Interpreting results

  • Compare total scores and item-level patterns from pre to post.
  • A higher score suggests stronger performance on these scenarios; it does not prove transfer to classroom practice.
  • Compare this assessment only with its own parallel form.
  • Do not combine these scores with the AI-literacy assessment, competency checklist, or capstone rubric. Those instruments measure different evidence and use different scales.
  • Use item-level results to select follow-up coaching.
  • A score of 12–14 with no zero on privacy or professional responsibility may be used as a local course-completion suggestion, not a credential or district evaluation standard.
