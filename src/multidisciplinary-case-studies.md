# Multidisciplinary Special Education Case Studies

These fictional cases broaden the course beyond the Expanded Core Curriculum (ECC) and vision-specific instruction. They are designed for special education teachers, teachers of deaf and hard-of-hearing students, school psychologists, speech-language pathologists (SLPs), related-service providers, and classroom partners.

Use one case that fits your professional role and scope of practice as the common thread through the modules. You may also select a different case for a particular lab. The cases are deliberately incomplete: the purpose is to practice identifying what is known, what is unknown, what must be observed, and which professional or team must make each decision.

**All names, histories, observations, and sample data are fictional.** They are not templates for actual students and must not be copied into student records. Do not infer disability, eligibility, placement, services, or intervention from a case label. Use only the facts supplied; identify missing information rather than filling gaps. For real work, follow district policy and use only approved systems and appropriately authorized information. Removing a name alone does not make student information de-identified.

## Case 1: Aureliano Buendia — vision, access, and dual-media learning

The [vision/ECC case study](07-fictional-student-case.md) remains the course's detailed worked example. Aureliano is a fictional student with ocular albinism and photophobia, corrected acuity of 20/175, glare sensitivity, glasses and brimmed-hat access needs, O&M instruction, emerging braille literacy as a dual-media reader, Windows Magnifier learning, and iPad use.

**Practice focus:** Select one bounded access task, such as navigating a digital handout with an agreed access method. Keep visual access, braille, print, assistive technology, O&M, and student-reported comfort distinct where they require different measures.

**Do not infer:** Functional vision, preferred literacy medium, fatigue, independence, or the need for a particular device or service from acuity, diagnosis, grades, or device ownership. Consult the learning media assessment, functional vision information, O&M professional, student, family, and team as appropriate.

## Case 2: Elena Rivera — deaf and hard-of-hearing education

Elena is a fictional middle-school student who uses hearing technology in some settings and visual communication supports in others. Her teachers report that she participates actively in small-group activities but misses portions of fast-paced whole-class discussions, especially when several people speak or when speakers are not facing the class. The team is reviewing how classroom communication access is planned and documented.

**Known facts**
- Elena uses hearing technology in some school settings.
- Staff report stronger participation in small groups than in fast-paced whole-class discussions.
- The team wants to examine access to classroom instruction and peer discussion.

**Unknowns**
- Elena's preferred communication approaches in different settings.
- Whether captions, interpreting, assistive listening technology, visual supports, seating, or speaker-turn routines are consistently available and effective.
- Whether the reported differences relate to acoustics, language demands, speaker behavior, group size, fatigue, technology function, or other factors.
- Elena's own account of access, effort, and participation.

**Practice task:** Design a brief classroom communication-access observation protocol. Define observable indicators such as access to speaker turns, availability and usability of agreed supports, opportunities to request clarification, and student-reported access. Record environmental and instructional conditions.

**Professional boundaries:** Do not infer language proficiency, cognitive ability, or comprehension from hearing status or use of a particular communication mode. Do not recommend changing communication modality, hearing technology, interpreting, or services based on this vignette. Those decisions require qualified professionals, student/family input, assessment, and the educational team. AI may help draft observation questions or organize synthetic notes; it cannot assess hearing, language, or communication access.

## Case 3: Jordan Lee — school psychology and problem-solving

Jordan is a fictional fourth-grade student referred to a school problem-solving team because classroom work completion and participation vary across tasks. Teachers report that Jordan contributes thoughtful ideas during discussion but often leaves multi-step independent assignments unfinished. Existing notes are inconsistent: some describe attention, others task difficulty, and others unclear directions. No formal evaluation findings are supplied.

**Known facts**
- Jordan contributes ideas during classroom discussion.
- Completion of multi-step independent assignments varies.
- Staff notes use inconsistent descriptions of the concern.
- The team wants clearer information before deciding what to do next.

**Unknowns**
- Whether task length, reading demands, working conditions, clarity of directions, instructional match, attendance, language, attention, or other variables are associated with the pattern.
- Jordan's perspective and strengths across settings.
- Which interventions have been implemented, at what intensity, and with what fidelity.
- Whether existing data are reliable, representative, and sufficient for any evaluation or eligibility question.

**Practice task:** Use the case to build a hypothesis-neutral problem-solving record. Define the target behavior or outcome operationally, identify multiple testable hypotheses, and design a plan for collecting comparable observations across relevant settings and tasks. Separate screening, progress monitoring, intervention response, and comprehensive evaluation questions.

**Professional boundaries:** An LLM must not diagnose, determine eligibility, select assessment instruments as a substitute for qualified judgment, interpret test protocols, or make placement/service recommendations. School psychologists and the multidisciplinary team retain responsibility for assessment design, interpretation, consultation, and decisions under applicable law and professional standards. AI can help organize fictional data, generate questions, and check whether a proposed observation plan is clear.

## Case 4: Samira Patel — speech-language pathology and functional communication

Samira is a fictional second-grade student who communicates using spoken language and an augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) system during some activities. Staff report that she initiates communication during preferred routines but participates less during open-ended academic discussions. Different adults describe her communication opportunities and prompting differently.

**Known facts**
- Samira uses spoken language and an AAC system in some activities.
- Staff report different levels of initiation across preferred routines and open-ended discussions.
- Adults do not use consistent descriptions of communication prompts or opportunities.

**Unknowns**
- Samira's communication preferences and access to her AAC system throughout the day.
- Whether vocabulary, device availability, partner wait time, modeling, sensory/environmental conditions, or activity structure affect opportunities.
- The functions and communication modes Samira uses across partners and settings.
- Whether the observation samples reflect typical communication and include valid opportunities.

**Practice task:** Create a communication-partner observation tool that records the context, communication opportunity, mode used, communicative function, partner modeling, wait time, prompt level, and outcome. Include a student-centered way to gather feedback and avoid treating speech as the only successful communication mode.

**Professional boundaries:** Do not diagnose a communication disorder, prescribe an AAC system, infer competence from speech output, or recommend reducing access to AAC. SLP assessment and clinical decisions remain with qualified professionals in collaboration with the student, family, and team. AI can help draft neutral observation categories and summarize synthetic records while preserving communication modes and context.

## Case 5: Marcus Chen — specific learning disability in mathematics reasoning

Marcus is a fictional fifth-grade student receiving special education support for a documented specific learning disability affecting mathematical problem solving and reasoning. He accurately computes many familiar single-step problems but has difficulty explaining how to represent multi-step word problems, selecting relevant information, and checking whether an answer is reasonable. The case label is supplied for instructional context; it is not an invitation to diagnose or re-evaluate Marcus.

**Known facts**
- Marcus receives special education support related to mathematics reasoning.
- He performs many familiar single-step computations accurately.
- Multi-step word problems create difficulty with representation, selecting relevant information, and checking reasonableness.

**Unknowns**
- Which problem structures, language features, representations, and prerequisite concepts are associated with stronger or weaker performance.
- Whether errors occur during comprehension, schema selection, representation, calculation, sequencing, or checking.
- The effect of explicit instruction, worked examples, visual or concrete representations, vocabulary support, and fading prompts.
- Baseline performance across comparable problem types and settings.

**Practice task:** Build a task analysis and data sheet that distinguishes stages of mathematical reasoning: identify the question, represent the situation, select relevant information, choose an operation or strategy, solve, and evaluate reasonableness. Track each stage separately and record prompt level, problem type, and representation used. Do not collapse reasoning and computation into one accuracy score.

**Professional boundaries:** Do not infer a new diagnosis, prescribe a placement, or change an IEP based on the case. Avoid assuming that a single strategy or representation is appropriate for every problem. Instructional decisions require review of work samples, curriculum expectations, progress data, student input, and the educator/IEP team's judgment.

## Case 6: Nia Williams — specific learning disability in reading

Nia is a fictional third-grade student receiving special education support for a documented specific learning disability affecting reading. She participates in class discussion and demonstrates strong background knowledge when information is presented orally. In reading tasks, her accuracy and fluency vary by text and task; staff want to understand the pattern without confusing decoding, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension.

**Known facts**
- Nia receives special education support related to reading.
- She demonstrates strong background knowledge in oral discussion.
- Reading accuracy and fluency vary across texts and tasks.
- Staff want more precise information about component skills and reading demands.

**Unknowns**
- Current performance and instructional history in phonological awareness, decoding, word recognition, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension, as applicable to the student's instructional program.
- Text difficulty, familiarity, language demands, accommodations, and whether measures are comparable.
- Nia's perspective on reading effort, confidence, interests, and strategies.
- Whether observed comprehension differences are associated with decoding demands, vocabulary, background knowledge, task format, or other factors.

**Practice task:** Design a reading progress-monitoring plan that identifies the specific construct being measured. Define the text selection and difficulty rules, timing or untimed conditions as appropriate, error coding, prompt rules, and separate measures for accuracy, fluency, and comprehension when needed. Use synthetic passages or approved materials; do not generate or administer assessment items as if they were standardized instruments.

**Professional boundaries:** Do not diagnose dyslexia or another condition, interpret standardized assessments, select a reading intervention solely from the vignette, or infer comprehension from oral discussion alone. Use qualified assessment, curriculum-based evidence, instructional history, student input, and team judgment. AI may help organize a fictional error analysis or draft neutral observation forms, but it cannot replace validated assessment or specialist expertise.

## Selecting and using a case across the modules

1. **Choose a role-relevant case.** Select the case closest to the work you do. A participant may work with a partner from another discipline, but each person should identify which decisions fall within their own role.
2. **Plan.** State one bounded task or question. List supplied facts, hypotheses to investigate, and unknowns separately.
3. **Refine.** Ask the model to identify missing evidence, alternative explanations, possible bias, and questions for the student and team. Correct the model's assumptions before proceeding.
4. **Build.** Create one small artifact aligned with the case: an observation protocol, task analysis, data sheet, meeting-question list, communication-access check, or progress-monitoring summary.
5. **Refine again.** Test the artifact against the case requirements, inspect accessibility, and revise it. Document what was retained, changed, rejected, and why.

## Reusable cross-case prompt

> I am a special education professional working with this fictional case. My role and scope of practice are: [state role]. The bounded task I want help with is: [one task]. Use only the facts supplied. Separate facts, hypotheses, and unknowns. Do not diagnose, determine eligibility, recommend placement or services, interpret standardized test results, or make an IEP-team decision. Identify missing evidence, questions for the student and relevant professionals, possible environmental or instructional variables, and a small draft artifact I can review. Mark all assumptions. Stop before making a professional or team decision.

## Cross-case quality check

Before accepting an AI-assisted artifact, confirm that it:
- Fits the learner, task, discipline, setting, and professional scope.
- Separates observation from interpretation and fact from hypothesis.
- Preserves student strengths, voice, communication preferences, and access needs.
- Defines opportunities, conditions, operational measures, and prompt levels where relevant.
- Does not treat diagnosis or eligibility labels as a complete learner profile.
- Does not replace validated assessment, clinical judgment, legal requirements, or IEP-team decisions.
- Uses fictional or appropriately authorized information in a district-approved system.
- Is usable with the intended person's keyboard, screen reader, AAC, hearing, visual, and other access workflows as relevant.
