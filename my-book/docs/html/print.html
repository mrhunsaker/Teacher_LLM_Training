Special Education Professional Learning: Practical LLM Use

Special Education Professional Learning: Practical LLM Use

This course teaches special education professionals to use large language models (LLMs) and Microsoft 365 Copilot as bounded, privacy-conscious productivity tools. It is designed for special education teachers, teachers of students with visual impairments (TVIs), teachers of deaf and hard-of-hearing students, school psychologists, speech-language pathologists (SLPs), occupational therapists (OTs), physical therapists (PTs), adapted physical education (APE) teachers, general education teachers, related-service providers, and classroom partners.

Participants choose one fictional case from the unified Buendía-family case collection and use it throughout Plan → Refine → Build → Refine. All ten cases have equal standing and use the same learning pathway; no discipline is the default. The course supports instructional planning, related-service workflows, IEP-related preparation, observation and progress monitoring, data review, critical evaluation, and professional communication.

Start here: the participant pathway

  1. Read Getting Started.
  2. Select one role-relevant case from the Buendía-family case collection. Use that case throughout the modules and workbook.
  3. Complete the applied-course pre-assessment before Session 1.
  4. Work through Sessions 1–6, using the Participant Lesson Handouts as a working packet.
  5. Complete the AI-literacy pre-assessment immediately before Session 7.
  6. Work through Sessions 7–9 and complete the independent capstone in Session 10.
  7. Complete both post-assessments after the capstone. Use the Practical Competency Checklist to identify strengths and coaching needs.

The Course Map and Assessment Plan is the definitive source for the ten-session sequence, activities, assessment timing, participant evidence, and completion expectations.

What participants will learn

By the end of the course, participants can:

  • Write bounded prompts and keep AI within a defined task and professional scope.
  • Separate facts, hypotheses, and unknowns; identify evidence and questions still needed.
  • Draft or review role-appropriate educational targets and operational measures.
  • Build aligned observation and progress-monitoring tools and verify spreadsheet calculations.
  • Interpret data cautiously, including variability, prompt dependence, comparability, and competing explanations.
  • Explain LLM limitations and identify hallucinations, omissions, false precision, and unsupported claims.
  • Verify sources, calculations, accessibility, and privacy before using an AI-assisted artifact.
  • Demonstrate Plan → Refine → Build → Refine and retain professional/team decision authority.

The ten-session sequence

Sessions 1–6: Applied workflows

  1. Prompting: Define a bounded task, role, context, constraints, output, and stop condition.
  2. Plan, Refine, and goal brainstorming: Separate facts, hypotheses, and unknowns; identify student/team questions; draft role-appropriate target options for review.
  3. Data collection: Define observable behaviors or outcomes, valid opportunities, prompt/support levels, and aligned data-collection tools.
  4. Excel progress monitoring: Enter synthetic data, verify formulas and calculations, and interpret the graph in text.
  5. Data-based decisions: Examine data quality, level, trend, variability, access conditions, and competing explanations.
  6. Copilot email triage: Produce a human-reviewed action report from simulated email; verify original messages and dates.

Sessions 7–10: AI literacy and independent performance

  1. AI literacy foundations: Understand LLM generation, context, variability, and appropriate tool choice.
  2. Misconceptions and failure analysis: Identify unsupported claims and correct flawed prompts and responses.
  3. Critical evaluation lab: Verify claims, calculations, citations, accessibility, privacy, and task fit.
  4. Independent performance capstone: Complete and explain an independently planned, built, refined, and verified artifact using a role-relevant fictional case.

Detailed agendas are in the six-session applied facilitator guide and four-session AI-literacy facilitator guide. Together they cover all ten sessions. The master course map resolves overall sequencing and assessment timing.

Assessment and evidence

There are two parallel written assessments and one performance capstone:

  • Participant Pre- and Post-Assessment: seven applied special-education scenarios; pre before Session 1 and post after Session 10.
  • AI Literacy and Misconception Assessment: five AI-literacy scenarios; pre before Session 7 and post after Session 10.
  • Module 10 Independent Performance Capstone: performance evidence scored with a separate 0–3 rubric during Session 10.

Compare each written assessment only with its own parallel form and review item-level patterns. The Practical Competency Checklist uses an item-level 0–2 scale for formative observation and coaching. Do not combine its scores with the capstone’s 0–3 rubric. None of these tools is a credential, employment evaluation, or proof of transfer to practice.

The collaborative design cycle

The course uses Plan → Refine → Build → Refine, not one-shot prompt generation:

  • Plan: Define the educational or related-service question, meaningful task, context, learner strengths, supplied facts, and unknowns.
  • Refine: Use the LLM to surface assumptions, missing information, and questions. The professional narrows and approves the plan.
  • Build: Draft one bounded artifact, such as a task analysis, observation form, communication-access check, or data summary.
  • Refine: Test the artifact, review evidence and learner/staff feedback, and revise it.

The professional remains responsible for decisions within their role. IEP, evaluation, eligibility, placement, service, clinical, and other reserved decisions remain with qualified professionals and authorized teams.

The role of the Expanded Core Curriculum

The Expanded Core Curriculum (ECC) is one specialized framework represented in this course, not its overall boundary. It supplements the general education curriculum with disability-specific skills that students with visual impairments may need to access information, participate meaningfully, and build independence. Its nine traditional areas are compensatory or functional academic skills (including communication modes), orientation and mobility, social interaction skills, independent living skills, recreation and leisure skills, career education, assistive technology, sensory efficiency, and self-determination.

The broader course also addresses communication access, academic instruction, school psychology problem-solving, related services, observation, progress monitoring, and cross-disciplinary collaboration. Instruction and services must be individualized; no learner is assumed to need every area.

Implementation and safety requirements

  • Use fictional or synthetic information for course practice. Removing a name alone does not establish de-identification.
  • For real work, use only specifically district-approved tools and data workflows; follow privacy, retention, sharing, and reporting requirements.
  • Do not infer functional vision, communication, behavior, cognition, literacy, independence, or educational need from a diagnosis, eligibility label, test score, acuity, grades, or device ownership alone.
  • Distinguish independent from prompted performance. Define valid opportunities, operational measures, accommodations, communication modes, and task conditions.
  • The greater-than-90%-across-three-sessions rule is a configurable training convention, not an IDEA or universal legal standard.
  • LLM output and spreadsheet flags are aids, not authorities. Verify claims, calculations, and source material.
  • Copilot email triage is advisory. Review original messages and deadlines; do not permit autonomous send, delete, archive, or commitments.
  • Do not delegate diagnosis, evaluation, eligibility, placement, services, IEP-team decisions, or professional/clinical judgment to AI.
  • Make accessibility checks part of the workflow. Text alternatives, semantic headings, keyboard access, and relevant screen-reader and communication workflows matter.

Build and accessibility verification

The book is built with mdBook. From the repository root, run mdbook build and inspect the rendered output. Before release, verify internal links, heading hierarchy, keyboard navigation, and representative content with the screen readers and access technologies used by participants. A successful build does not by itself establish accessibility; record the actual test environment and results.

How to interpret assessment evidence

  • Compare each written assessment only with its own parallel form: applied pre vs. applied post; AI-literacy pre vs. AI-literacy post.
  • Both written assessments score each response 0–2. These are knowledge/reasoning ratings, not observations of performance.
  • The competency checklist also uses 0–2, but rates individual observable behaviors during practice. Do not add its ratings to either written assessment.
  • The capstone uses a separate 0–3 domain rubric to score the independent performance demonstration. Do not convert or combine this scale with either 0–2 system.
  • Review item-level patterns and evidence, not only totals. Written assessments measure scenario-based reasoning; the capstone provides performance evidence under course conditions. Neither proves transfer to practice or professional certification.
  • Use the competency checklist formatively during practice and coaching.
  • Scores are learning evidence, not employment evaluations or credentials.

For the standard ten-session course, deliver Sessions 7–10 as four distinct sessions. Decision boundaries

AI may help brainstorm, organize, draft, check consistency, and identify questions. It does not diagnose, determine eligibility, interpret standardized assessments in place of qualified professionals, decide placement or services, make IEP-team decisions, or replace clinical, instructional, or school-psychology judgment. A decision not to use AI is an acceptable and sometimes preferred outcome.

Release validation

Completion and transfer

Completion means the participant has completed the learning activities and submitted the expected evidence. It does not certify professional competence or prove transfer. A voluntary or locally approved follow-up 4–6 weeks later can examine whether the participant applies the workflow safely with fictional or approved information, verifies output, tests accessibility, and documents professional judgment.

Getting Started

What you need

  • Microsoft 365 Copilot in Outlook, if your district license and administrator have enabled the relevant features.

A simple working routine

  1. Read the module before opening an AI tool.
  2. Copy the prompt into the approved tool.
  3. Replace bracketed fields only with fictional or permitted information.
  4. Review the response against the checklist.
  5. Save only the educator-reviewed product in the district-approved location.
  6. Record what was changed and why.

Privacy: de-identification is more than removing a name

Remove direct identifiers and indirect details that could reasonably identify a student. Replace names with neutral labels such as “Learner A.” Generalize dates, locations, rare diagnoses, unique events, and family details. Do not upload IEP documents, evaluation reports, medical records, photographs, or raw email threads unless the district has explicitly approved that exact system and workflow for that information.

A prompt that says “this is confidential” does not make an unapproved tool safe. Follow district policy, the district’s data-processing terms, and applicable law. When uncertain, stop and ask the district privacy or technology lead.

A note about the 90% criterion

For consistency with this training request, the sample spreadsheet uses a rule of more than 90% accuracy on at least three distinct qualifying sessions. In practice, the team must decide whether that threshold, number of sessions, independence level, and generalization conditions are appropriate for the specific skill. Some skills require safety, fluency, consistency across settings, or another measure rather than a simple percentage. Never retrofit the goal to the spreadsheet.

How to use the prompts

Prompts are templates, not magic phrases. Keep the task narrow. Give the model only the information needed. Ask it to label assumptions and missing information. If it starts doing extra work, stop and restate the boundary rather than accepting an expanded answer.

This is the course’s single case-study collection and worked-practice pathway. Choose the case that best fits your professional role, current learning goal, or area of practice. Naming convention: The first six case names follow the Buendía family sequence already established in the course. Service-category note: Under IDEA, occupational therapy and physical therapy are listed as related services when required to assist a child with a disability to benefit from special education (34 CFR § 300.34). Physical education includes special physical education and adapted physical education in the definition of special education (34 CFR § 300.39(b)(2)); physical education must be available and specially designed when necessary (34 CFR § 300.108). Accordingly, the APE case focuses on adapted PE instruction and access; it does not label APE automatically as a related service. Follow state and district terminology and requirements.

Fictional-data safeguard: All student profiles, histories, observations, and sample data below are invented. They are not templates for actual students and must not be copied into student records. Do not infer disability, eligibility, placement, services, intervention, or learner characteristics from a case label. Use only supplied facts; identify missing information rather than filling gaps. For real work, follow district policy and use only approved systems and appropriately authorized information. Removing a name alone does not make student information de-identified.

Choose one continuing case

Case 1: Úrsula — vision, access, and dual-media learning

Úrsula is a fictional student with ocular albinism and photophobia. With corrective lenses, her measured visual acuity is 20/175. She wears glasses and uses a brimmed hat to reduce the impact of overhead lighting. She is highly sensitive to glare. Her academic performance is solidly in the B range.

Úrsula receives orientation and mobility (O&M) instruction. She is learning braille as a second literacy medium as a dual-media reader. For visual access, she is learning Windows Magnifier; ZoomText may be considered only if the team identifies a need for features not met by built-in magnification. She also uses an iPad in class.

The instructional question is not how to improve her grades. It is how to make access efficient, comfortable, independent, and sustainable across environments, materials, and tasks while developing literacy and technology options.

Known facts

  • Ocular albinism and photophobia; corrected acuity of 20/175.
  • Glasses and a brimmed hat are established supports; glare sensitivity is reported.
  • O&M instruction is in place.
  • She is developing braille as a second literacy medium and learning Windows Magnifier.
  • She uses an iPad in class and earns generally solid grades.

Unknowns — do not assume

  • Functional vision performance at different distances, lighting levels, and task types.
  • Which glare sources and environments are most disruptive, and which adjustments are effective.
  • Current braille readiness, tactile skills, reading fluency, and contexts where braille is most useful.
  • Magnifier proficiency, preferred settings, task efficiency, and whether built-in tools meet access needs.
  • Which iPad apps, accessibility settings, workflows, and file formats are effective.
  • Whether grades conceal extra time, effort, adult assistance, or reduced access.

Practice task: Select one bounded access task, such as opening and navigating a digital classroom handout using an agreed access method. Define observable steps and record prompt level, task conditions, access method, and student-reported comfort and efficiency. Keep visual access, braille, print, assistive technology, O&M, and student feedback distinct when they require different measures.

Professional boundaries: Do not infer functional vision needs or select a primary literacy medium from acuity, diagnosis, grades, or device ownership. Use the learning media assessment, functional vision information, learning media plan, observations across settings, qualified clinical guidance where appropriate, and student/family input. O&M assessment and travel decisions remain with the qualified O&M professional. An LLM cannot perform these assessments or make team decisions.

Case 2: José Arcadio — deaf and hard-of-hearing education

José Arcadio is a fictional middle-school student who uses hearing technology in some settings and visual communication supports in others. Teachers report that he participates actively in small-group activities but misses portions of fast-paced whole-class discussions, especially when several people speak or when speakers are not facing the class. The team is reviewing how classroom communication access is planned and documented.

Known facts

  • He uses hearing technology in some school settings.
  • Staff report stronger participation in small groups than in fast-paced whole-class discussions.
  • The team wants to examine access to instruction and peer discussion.

Unknowns — do not assume

  • His preferred communication approaches in different settings.
  • Whether captions, interpreting, assistive listening technology, visual supports, seating, or speaker-turn routines are consistently available and effective.
  • Whether differences relate to acoustics, language demands, speaker behavior, group size, fatigue, technology function, or other factors.
  • His own account of access, effort, and participation.

Practice task: Design a brief classroom communication-access observation protocol. Define observable indicators such as access to speaker turns, availability and usability of agreed supports, opportunities to request clarification, and student-reported access. Record environmental and instructional conditions.

Professional boundaries: Do not infer language proficiency, cognitive ability, or comprehension from hearing status or communication mode. Do not recommend changing communication modality, hearing technology, interpreting, or services based on this vignette. Those decisions require qualified professionals, student/family input, assessment, and the educational team.

Case 3: Aureliano — school psychology and problem-solving

Aureliano is a fictional fourth-grade student referred to a school problem-solving team because classroom work completion and participation vary across tasks. Teachers report that he contributes thoughtful ideas during discussion but often leaves multi-step independent assignments unfinished. Existing notes are inconsistent: some describe attention, others task difficulty, and others unclear directions. No formal evaluation findings are supplied.

Known facts

  • He contributes ideas during classroom discussion.
  • Completion of multi-step independent assignments varies.
  • Staff notes use inconsistent descriptions of the concern.
  • The team wants clearer information before deciding what to do next.

Unknowns — do not assume

  • Whether task length, reading demands, working conditions, clarity of directions, instructional match, attendance, language, attention, or other variables are associated with the pattern.
  • His perspective and strengths across settings.
  • Which interventions have been implemented, at what intensity, and with what fidelity.
  • Whether existing data are reliable, representative, and sufficient for any evaluation or eligibility question.

Practice task: Build a hypothesis-neutral problem-solving record. Define the target behavior or outcome operationally, identify multiple testable hypotheses, and design a plan for comparable observations across relevant settings and tasks. Separate screening, progress monitoring, intervention response, and comprehensive evaluation questions.

Professional boundaries: An LLM must not diagnose, determine eligibility, select assessment instruments as a substitute for qualified judgment, interpret test protocols, or make placement/service recommendations. Qualified school psychologists and the multidisciplinary team retain responsibility for assessment design, interpretation, consultation, and decisions under applicable law and professional standards.

Case 4: Amaranta — speech-language pathology and functional communication

Amaranta is a fictional second-grade student who communicates using spoken language and an augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) system during some activities. Staff report that she initiates communication during preferred routines but participates less during open-ended academic discussions. Different adults describe her communication opportunities and prompting differently.

Known facts

  • She uses spoken language and an AAC system in some activities.
  • Staff report different levels of initiation across preferred routines and open-ended discussions.
  • Adults do not use consistent descriptions of communication prompts or opportunities.

Unknowns — do not assume

  • Her communication preferences and access to her AAC system throughout the day.
  • Whether vocabulary, device availability, partner wait time, modeling, sensory/environmental conditions, or activity structure affect opportunities.
  • The functions and communication modes she uses across partners and settings.
  • Whether observation samples reflect typical communication and include valid opportunities.

Practice task: Create a communication-partner observation tool that records context, communication opportunity, mode used, communicative function, partner modeling, wait time, prompt level, and outcome. Include a student-centered way to gather feedback; do not treat speech as the only successful communication mode.

Professional boundaries: Do not diagnose a communication disorder, prescribe an AAC system, infer competence from speech output, or recommend reducing AAC access. SLP assessment and clinical decisions remain with qualified professionals in collaboration with the student, family, and team.

Case 5: Rebeca — specific learning disability in mathematics reasoning

Rebeca is a fictional fifth-grade student receiving special education support for a documented specific learning disability affecting mathematical problem solving and reasoning. She accurately computes many familiar single-step problems but has difficulty explaining how to represent multi-step word problems, selecting relevant information, and checking whether an answer is reasonable. The case label is supplied for instructional context; it is not an invitation to diagnose or re-evaluate her.

Known facts

  • She receives special education support related to mathematics reasoning.
  • She performs many familiar single-step computations accurately.
  • Multi-step word problems create difficulty with representation, selecting relevant information, and checking reasonableness.

Unknowns — do not assume

  • Which problem structures, language features, representations, and prerequisite concepts are associated with stronger or weaker performance.
  • Whether errors occur during comprehension, schema selection, representation, calculation, sequencing, or checking.
  • The effect of explicit instruction, worked examples, visual or concrete representations, vocabulary support, and fading prompts.
  • Baseline performance across comparable problem types and settings.

Practice task: Build a task analysis and data sheet that distinguishes stages of mathematical reasoning: identify the question, represent the situation, select relevant information, choose an operation or strategy, solve, and evaluate reasonableness. Track each stage separately and record prompt level, problem type, and representation. Do not collapse reasoning and computation into one accuracy score.

Module 1: Prompting an LLM with Clear Boundaries

Learning targets

  • Build a prompt with a defined task, context, constraints, output format, and stop condition.
  • Prevent the model from expanding the task or inventing facts.
  • Review the output before using it.
  • Use prompting as part of a collaborative design cycle rather than a one-time request for a finished plan.

The course design cycle: Plan → Refine → Build → Refine

A prompt is one move in a professional workflow, not a substitute for planning. The educator or qualified service provider owns the sequence and approves each transition.

  1. Plan: State the educational or related-service question, relevant discipline or framework, meaningful task, learner strengths, known facts, and unknowns. Ask the model to organize the problem—not solve every adjacent problem.
  2. Refine: Ask the model to challenge assumptions, identify missing observations, and compare possible task definitions. Correct errors and approve a narrow direction.
  3. Build: Request one concrete artifact, such as a task analysis, data sheet, checklist, or draft lesson component.
  4. Refine: Test the artifact in context, collect evidence and student feedback, then ask the model to help identify needed revisions. Repeat as needed.

Do not let the model move from brainstorming to goal writing, service recommendations, or instructional programming unless the responsible professional explicitly requests that next step within their role and authority. A useful collaboration includes teacher edits, rejected suggestions, and a brief record of why the plan changed.

The six-part prompt

  1. Role: What perspective should the model use? Example: “You are a planning assistant supporting a special education professional. My role and scope of practice are [state role].”
  2. Task: One action only. Example: “Create three possible ways to measure this skill.”
  3. Context: Only the minimum relevant background.
  4. Constraints: What it must and must not do.
  5. Output format: A table, short list, or specific headings.
  6. Stop condition: Tell it exactly when to stop and what not to add.

Copy-ready bounded prompt

Context: [Paste only fictional or district-approved de-identified information needed for this task.]

Vague: “Make a complete special education plan for this student.”

Bounded: “Using only the fictional case facts and synthetic baseline below, help me clarify one measurable educational or related-service target within my professional role: [state role]. Do not diagnose, determine eligibility, select services or placement, or choose the final target for the team. Stop after the options and questions.”

Follow-up questions without scope creep

Use a separate prompt for each step:

  • “Ask me what the current baseline is. Do not draft a goal yet.”
  • “Using the baseline I supplied, identify possible measurable behaviors. Do not write objectives.”
  • “Now draft two goal options using the measurement conditions I specify. Do not choose between them.”
  • “Check this draft for measurability only. Do not rewrite it.”

Quick scoring rules

  • Independent accuracy = independent correct ÷ valid opportunities × 100.

Module 5: Interpreting Progress, Growth, and Possible Plateaus

Learning targets

  • Describe a data pattern accurately without overclaiming.
  • Distinguish limited data, variable performance, slow progress, and a possible plateau.
  • Use an LLM to organize questions while retaining professional judgment.

Read the graph in context

Review these features:

  • Level: Typical performance and current range.
  • Trend: Direction and rate of change over time.
  • Variability: How much performance changes from session to session.
  • Data density: Number and spacing of valid sessions.
  • Independence: Whether performance is unprompted or supported.
  • Generalization: Whether the skill appears with different people, materials, routines, or environments.
  • Maintenance: Whether performance persists after time or reduced teaching.
  • Fidelity and access: Whether instruction and required access supports were consistently available.

A single low score is not a plateau. Three identical points do not automatically establish a plateau. A flat trend may reflect insufficient opportunities, inconsistent access, changing task difficulty, absence, measurement error, or instruction that was not delivered as planned.

A cautious plateau review

Before using the word “plateau,” ask:

  1. Are there enough recent, valid, comparable data points to identify a trend?
  2. Was the same operational definition used?
  3. Were opportunities and access supports consistent?
  4. Was instruction delivered as planned and at an adequate frequency?
  5. Are prompt levels changing even when accuracy is stable?
  6. Is the skill too easy, too difficult, or measured in a way that misses meaningful progress?
  7. Does the learner demonstrate the skill in another context?
  8. Have student voice, fatigue, sensory access, motivation, and environmental barriers been considered?

Use district or team procedures to define the review window. This course does not impose a universal number of sessions for declaring a plateau.

Safe LLM analysis prompt

You are a data-description assistant for a special education professional. Analyze only the synthetic, de-identified session data below.

Goal and measurement definition: [Paste exact goal and operational definition.]

Data: [Paste date/session, opportunities, independent correct, prompted correct, setting, and relevant access notes.]

Task: Describe the observed level, direction of trend, variability, and any differences between independent and prompted performance. Identify data limitations and plausible questions for the instructional team. State whether the pattern is consistent with growth, variable performance, insufficient evidence, or a possible plateau; use cautious language and explain the evidence.

Boundaries: Do not diagnose, determine eligibility, change an IEP or service plan, recommend service minutes, infer causes, or invent missing data. Do not treat correlation as cause. If data are insufficient, say so. Do not declare mastery solely because a percentage threshold was reached.

Output: 1) factual summary, 2) limitations, 3) questions to investigate, 4) possible next data to collect. Stop.

Module 7 — AI Literacy Foundations: What Generative AI Does and Does Not Do

Purpose

This module gives educators a practical mental model of generative artificial intelligence (AI), especially large language models (LLMs). The goal is not to teach computer science. The goal is to help teachers predict common failure modes, choose appropriate tasks, and evaluate outputs before using them in professional work.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, participants can:

  • Explain in plain language what an LLM does when it generates a response.
  • Distinguish an LLM from a search engine, a database, and a rules-based calculator.
  • Explain why a fluent answer may still be incorrect, incomplete, biased, or fabricated.
  • Identify how context, prompt design, model settings, and available tools affect outputs.
  • Describe why model responses can vary across attempts.
  • Decide when an AI tool adds value and when not using it is the better choice.

A practical mental model

A large language model is a system trained on large collections of data to recognize patterns in language and generate likely continuations in response to input. It processes the prompt and available context and produces a sequence of tokens (small units of text or other data). The output can be useful because the model has learned many patterns and relationships. It is not proof that the model has checked the world, understood a learner as a person, or verified every statement.

A useful shorthand is:

  • The prompt supplies a task and context.
  • The model generates a response based on learned patterns and the context available to it.
  • Additional tools, such as web search, file retrieval, or spreadsheet functions, may supply external information or calculations when enabled.
  • The educator checks the result against authoritative sources, observations, policy, and professional knowledge.

Do not assume every chatbot response used live web search, read a file, remembered a previous conversation, or ran a calculation. Ask what sources or tools were actually used, and verify important claims independently.

LLMs, search engines, databases, and calculators

LLM: Generates language and other supported content from patterns and supplied context. It can summarize, draft, reorganize, brainstorm, and help inspect information. It can also invent plausible details.

Search engine: Retrieves and ranks indexed sources. Search results still require source evaluation; retrieval does not guarantee truth.

Database: Stores structured records. A database query returns records that match the query and access permissions; it does not independently interpret their educational meaning.

Calculator or spreadsheet formula: Executes specified mathematical operations. The result can still be wrong if the formula, input data, or interpretation is wrong.

Some AI products combine these capabilities. The interface may look like one assistant, but the underlying operations are different. Identify whether a task needs generation, retrieval, calculation, or a combination.

Why fluent answers can be wrong

LLMs are optimized to generate plausible responses, not to guarantee factual correctness. Errors may include:

  • Hallucination: invented facts, citations, policy statements, or details presented confidently.
  • Omission: leaving out a relevant constraint, caveat, or piece of context.
  • Overgeneralization: applying a common pattern to a learner or setting where it does not fit.
  • False precision: supplying exact numbers, dates, or ratings unsupported by the input.
  • Assumption laundering: turning a tentative hypothesis into a statement that sounds like an observed fact.
  • Outdated information: describing features, laws, guidance, or product behavior that has changed.
  • Bias or representational gaps: reflecting limitations or stereotypes in data and system design.
  • Instruction-following failure: ignoring a constraint, changing the task, or inventing missing information.

A confident tone, polished format, or citation-like string does not establish reliability.

Why outputs vary

Responses can differ because of prompt wording, conversation context, model version, system instructions, sampling settings, connected tools, retrieved sources, and product updates. A repeated prompt may produce a different draft. Variation is not itself evidence that one answer is correct.

For consequential tasks, make the process reproducible: preserve the prompt, input definitions, tool/source details, date, output, and educator revisions where district policy permits. Verify results rather than relying on repeatability alone.

Context windows and missing information

An AI assistant can only use information available in the current interaction or through explicitly enabled memory, files, or connected tools. Long conversations may exceed context limits or have information summarized. The model may not know that an important fact is missing.

Teach the model to label:

  • facts explicitly supplied,
  • reasonable interpretations,
  • hypotheses to investigate,
  • unknowns that must remain unknown.

The educator should not ask the model to fill a gap with a guess.

Prompt quality is task design

A good prompt does not simply add more words. It defines a bounded task:

  1. Task: one action the model should perform.
  2. Context: only information relevant to that action.
  3. Constraints: what it must not infer, decide, or do.
  4. Output: format and level of detail needed.
  5. Verification: claims, calculations, or sources that need checking.
  6. Stop condition: where the model must pause and ask before proceeding.

The Plan → Refine → Build → Refine process applies these controls iteratively. The teacher remains in charge of scope and decisions.

When not to use AI

Do not use an AI tool when:

  • The tool or data use has not been approved for the information involved.
  • The task requires a professional determination the model is not qualified or authorized to make.
  • A direct observation, conversation with the learner, or consultation with a qualified colleague is more appropriate.
  • The output cannot be verified in the available time.
  • The task is too sensitive, high-stakes, or poorly defined for the available safeguards.
  • The tool’s accessibility, security, or reliability is inadequate for the task.
  • AI adds complexity without meaningful benefit.

Choosing not to use AI is a valid and sometimes preferred professional decision.

Demonstration: same task, different operation

Consider the request: “Tell me whether this student has mastered digital access.”

That request asks for an unsupported educational conclusion. A bounded alternative might be:

“Using only the fictional session data below, organize the recorded performance by session. Keep independent and prompted responses separate. Identify missing information and patterns that warrant educator review. Do not determine mastery, infer a disability-related need, or recommend a change to an IEP. Stop after listing the observations and questions for the educator.”

If arithmetic is needed, verify it with a spreadsheet or calculator. If current product guidance is needed, consult the current official documentation. If the student’s actual performance is at issue, consult valid observations and the student/team.

Guided activity: identify the operation

For each task, identify whether the primary need is generation, retrieval, calculation, direct observation, or professional judgment.

  1. Draft three alternative task-analysis formats from an educator-approved skill definition.
  2. Check the current Microsoft documentation for a feature’s availability.
  3. Calculate the percentage of independent steps completed across sessions.
  4. Determine whether glare is interfering with a student’s access in a particular classroom.
  5. Decide whether a proposed annual goal is appropriate for an individual learner.

Suggested responses: 1 generation; 2 retrieval; 3 calculation; 4 direct observation and assessment; 5 professional judgment and IEP-team process. Some tasks combine operations, but the distinction helps determine what must be verified.

Knowledge check

  1. Why can a fluent LLM answer still be false?
  2. What is the difference between generating a response and retrieving a current source?
  3. Name two reasons repeated responses may vary.
  4. What should the model do when a necessary fact is missing?
  5. Give one situation in which not using AI is the better choice.

Exit ticket

Complete these statements:

  • An LLM is useful for…
  • An LLM cannot establish…
  • Before I rely on an output, I will verify…
  • I will choose not to use AI when…

Facilitator notes

Avoid teaching a deterministic or overly technical account of model internals. Explain that products and models differ and change. Emphasize the distinction between language generation and verification. Ask participants to provide examples from their own professional workflows, but use fictional or approved data only. Correct the misconception that a single universal description applies to every AI product.

Module 8 — AI Misconceptions and Failure Analysis

Purpose

Teachers need repeated practice recognizing when AI output is unsupported, unsafe, or outside the requested scope. This module turns common misconceptions into observable skills: participants inspect model responses, mark evidence and assumptions, revise prompts, and explain what a qualified educator must decide.

Learning outcomes

Participants will be able to:

  • Identify common misconceptions about generative AI.
  • Distinguish evidence in the prompt from model-added claims.
  • Detect fabricated facts, false precision, missing caveats, and unsupported recommendations.
  • Revise a prompt or output to restore boundaries.
  • Explain why student-centered professional judgment cannot be delegated to an LLM.

Misconceptions to correct explicitly

Misconception 1: “AI understands a student like a teacher does.”

Correction: An LLM processes supplied information and generates patterns in language. It does not directly observe the learner, know their preferences, or understand the full educational context. A profile is not a substitute for assessment, observation, and student voice.

Misconception 2: “If it sounds confident, it is probably right.”

Correction: Fluency and confidence are not evidence. Require verification for factual, technical, legal, mathematical, and product-specific claims.

Misconception 3: “AI can determine the right IEP goal.”

Correction: AI can help brainstorm wording, identify missing measurement elements, or compare a draft with supplied criteria. Qualified professionals and the IEP team retain responsibility for evaluation, goals, services, placement, and decisions.

Misconception 4: “AI can interpret student data without context.”

Correction: A number is not self-interpreting. Check definitions, valid opportunities, denominator, prompts, accommodations, setting, materials, fidelity, and data quality. Do not combine prompted and independent performance as if equivalent.

Misconception 5: “AI-generated materials are automatically accessible.”

Correction: Accessibility must be designed and tested. Check semantic headings, reading order, keyboard operation, screen-reader output, document structure, contrast where visual presentation is used, and nonvisual alternatives. A text response may still be poorly structured or inaccessible.

Misconception 6: “AI remembers everything and consistently.”

Correction: Available context, memory, files, connected tools, and retention vary by product and settings. Do not assume that an assistant has access to a prior interaction or that it will preserve details correctly.

Misconception 7: “Removing the student’s name makes the data anonymous.”

Correction: Combinations of school, grade, disability, schedule, location, family details, rare events, or other attributes may identify a student. Use fictional data in general-purpose tools. Follow district approval and privacy procedures.

Misconception 8: “More prompt detail always produces a better answer.”

Correction: Relevant context helps; unnecessary detail can add privacy risk and distract from the task. A bounded prompt with a clear output and stop condition is preferable to a long unstructured profile.

Misconception 9: “AI can replace specialized TVI knowledge.”

Correction: AI does not replace learning media assessment, functional vision assessment, O&M expertise, assistive technology evaluation, student/family input, or professional collaboration.

Misconception 10: “A spreadsheet flag or model summary proves mastery or plateau.”

Correction: A flag applies a rule to entered data. A summary describes patterns in available information. Neither independently establishes mastery, causation, plateau, or the correct instructional response.

The evidence–inference–unknown method

When reviewing any AI response, mark each important statement as one of the following:

  • E — Evidence: explicitly present in the supplied information or verified source.
  • I — Inference: interpretation or hypothesis that may be useful but requires review.
  • U — Unknown: not established by available information.
  • X — Unsupported/error: invented, contradicted, or stated with unjustified certainty.

A participant should be able to explain the evidence for each E, the uncertainty around each I, and why each U must remain open. X statements must be removed or corrected before use.

Failure-analysis lab: fictional student profile

Use only the fictional information below.

  • A learner uses a tablet for some classroom activities.
  • The learner has a visual impairment.
  • The learner earns mostly Bs.
  • The learner completes familiar routines in one classroom.
  • No information is supplied about the student’s preferred access methods, efficiency, fatigue, performance in unfamiliar settings, or level of prompting.

Deliberately flawed AI response

The following response is intentionally incorrect and must not be used as educational advice.

“The student is clearly independent with technology because they own and use a tablet and earn good grades. Since the student has a visual impairment, they should use magnification for all digital tasks. The IEP should include a goal to complete every digital assignment with 95% accuracy independently within one month. The student does not need additional assessment because classroom grades are strong.”

Participant task

Annotate every sentence using E, I, U, or X. Then answer:

  1. Which statements are directly supported by the fictional profile?
  2. Which statements make unsupported assumptions about access, independence, or technology?
  3. What relevant information is missing?
  4. What questions would you ask the learner?
  5. Which assessments, observations, or team members could provide valid evidence?
  6. Rewrite the AI request so that the model identifies unknowns and generates questions rather than deciding a medium or goal.
  7. Describe what the educator would verify before using any draft.

Facilitator analysis

  • Tablet use is a supplied fact; it does not establish independent access.
  • Grades do not establish efficient access to digital materials or performance in all settings.
  • The student’s preferred or effective medium is unknown. Magnification for every task is an unsupported prescription.
  • No baseline, task definition, valid opportunities, prompt definitions, conditions, or rationale support the proposed 95% criterion or one-month timeline.
  • The claim that assessment is unnecessary is unsupported and inappropriate.
  • The response confuses a plausible narrative with evidence and makes decisions outside the model’s role.

Accept participant answers that distinguish what is known from what needs investigation. Do not require a specific access method or goal.

Failure-analysis lab: invented source and false precision

A second intentionally flawed response says:

“According to the 2025 National TVI AI Standard, all students with corrected acuity below 20/200 must use braille for at least 60% of assignments. The learner’s 92% score proves mastery.”

Participants should flag the named standard as unverified, the universal rule as unsupported, and the conclusion as invalid without knowing what the score measures, how it was collected, and under what conditions. The facilitator should model checking authoritative sources rather than asking another model to confirm the claim.

Prompt repair practice

Use this repair frame:

“Review the fictional information below. Do not diagnose, infer a preferred learning medium, determine eligibility, recommend services, or write an IEP goal. Separate supplied facts, possible hypotheses, and unknowns. Identify missing observations and questions for the student and team. Explain what evidence would be needed before an educator could consider a target. Use plain language and stop after the questions.”

Participants should adapt the frame to one of the other course cases.

Misconception check: true, false, or depends

  1. “An LLM response with citations is verified.” — False. Check that each source exists and supports the claim.
  2. “A model may generate different wording for the same prompt.” — True. Variation is expected in many systems.
  3. “A student’s device ownership proves independent access.” — False.
  4. “An AI tool may be useful for drafting questions for a team meeting.” — True, with review and privacy safeguards.
  5. “Removing names always makes student data safe to paste.” — False.
  6. “A model can calculate a percentage correctly if given clean data.” — Depends. It may calculate, but verify the formula, denominator, and result with a trusted calculation method.
  7. “A teacher may decide not to use AI.” — True.
  8. “AI-generated content is automatically accessible to a screen reader.” — False.
  9. “A high post-assessment score proves transfer to professional practice.” — False. Transfer requires observation of performance in relevant settings.
  10. “The teacher remains accountable for reviewing AI-assisted work.” — True.

Exit ticket

Write one AI claim you would no longer accept without verification. State what evidence you would seek and what action you would take if it could not be verified.

Facilitator notes

Make errors visible without shaming participants. Ask “What evidence supports that sentence?” rather than “Who got it wrong?” Do not let a flawed sample become a template. Keep all practice profiles fictional. If participants mention a real student, redirect them to a synthetic example and follow district reporting procedures if an actual privacy incident has occurred.

Module 9 — Critical Evaluation Lab: Verify, Revise, or Reject

Purpose

This lab develops a repeatable review routine for AI-assisted work. Participants practice checking factual claims, calculations, educational reasoning, accessibility, privacy, and alignment with the task before adopting any output.

Learning outcomes

Participants will:

  • Apply an evaluation sequence to AI-generated work.
  • Check claims against appropriate authoritative sources or raw data.
  • Identify missing context and unsupported assumptions.
  • Revise or reject an output when verification fails.
  • Document the educator’s contribution and final decision.

The six-step review sequence

  1. Scope: Does the response answer only the task requested? Did it cross a stop condition?
  2. Evidence: Which statements are supported by the prompt, observations, raw data, or verified sources?
  3. Accuracy: Are calculations, citations, dates, product details, and policy claims correct?
  4. Fit: Does the response fit this learner, task, setting, access needs, and instructional purpose?
  5. Accessibility and privacy: Is the output usable with relevant access technologies, and was the information handled in an approved way?
  6. Decision: Retain, revise, reject, or gather more evidence. Record why.

Module 10 — Independent Performance Capstone

Purpose

The capstone determines whether participants can apply the course practices independently in a bounded, fictional scenario. It assesses educator judgment, not the volume of AI use or the sophistication of prompt terminology.

A participant may demonstrate strong professional judgment by deciding not to use AI for a task. The capstone is a local learning assessment, not a credential, employment evaluation, or authorization to use a tool or student data.

Performance outcomes

The participant will independently:

  1. Define a bounded task and write a prompt with context, constraints, output requirements, and a stop condition.
  2. Separate supplied facts, hypotheses, and unknowns.
  3. Review a deliberately flawed AI response and identify unsupported claims.
  4. Create or refine one useful professional artifact.
  5. Verify factual claims and calculations using appropriate sources or raw data.
  6. explain accessibility and privacy safeguards relevant to the artifact.
  7. State which decisions remain with the educator, qualified professionals, or IEP team.
  8. Document what was retained, revised, rejected, and why.

Select a role-relevant scenario

Choose one case from the unified Buendía-family case collection. Use the bounded task and relevant evidence from that case. All ten cases are equal options; there is no default discipline-specific scenario. Preserve the same performance outcomes and scoring rubric regardless of the selected case.

Examples of equivalent bounded capstone tasks include:

  • Deaf and hard-of-hearing education: audit a fictional classroom communication-access observation plan; identify missing information about communication access, agreed supports, and student feedback.
  • School psychology: review fictional multi-setting classroom observations; separate observable behavior from interpretation and identify competing hypotheses without diagnosing or deciding eligibility.
  • Speech-language pathology: review synthetic communication samples or opportunity records; preserve communication modes, functions, partner supports, and prompt definitions without prescribing an AAC system or making a clinical decision.
  • Mathematics reasoning: inspect synthetic multi-step problem-solving data; distinguish comprehension, representation, strategy selection, computation, and checking rather than collapsing them into one score.
  • Reading: inspect synthetic reading data; distinguish the construct measured (for example, decoding, accuracy, fluency, vocabulary, or comprehension) and verify that materials and conditions are comparable.
  • Vision and access: use Úrsula’s case to examine one bounded access task, if that case fits your role.

For every option, participants must use only supplied fictional facts, identify unknowns, create one appropriate artifact, verify claims or calculations, test accessibility, and state which decisions remain with qualified professionals or the IEP team.

Scoring rubric

Score each domain 0–3. Maximum: 24.

  • 0 — Unsafe/not demonstrated: materially unsupported, inaccurate, or delegates professional decisions.
  • 1 — Beginning: identifies some issues but needs substantial prompting or misses important safeguards.
  • 2 — Competent with minor support: generally accurate and bounded; one or more details need refinement.
  • 3 — Independent demonstration: accurate, complete for the task, explicit about uncertainty, and defensible without prompting.

Domain 1: Bounded task and prompt

  • 0: asks AI to decide mastery, access method, or IEP changes.
  • 1: task is broad or missing important constraints.
  • 2: task, context, output, and most boundaries are clear.
  • 3: one task, relevant context, explicit prohibitions, verification, output format, and stop condition are all clear.

Domain 2: Facts, hypotheses, and unknowns

  • 0: treats assumptions as facts.
  • 1: identifies some facts but leaves important assumptions unmarked.
  • 2: separates most facts and unknowns.
  • 3: consistently distinguishes evidence, inference, and unknowns and includes student voice.

Domain 3: Failure detection

  • 0: accepts flawed claims.
  • 1: identifies an obvious error only.
  • 2: identifies most unsupported conclusions and explains some reasons.
  • 3: identifies false precision, causal claims, unsupported comparison, and premature IEP recommendations with clear reasoning.

Domain 4: Artifact quality

  • 0: artifact is unusable or creates risk.
  • 1: partially useful but lacks definitions or alignment.
  • 2: usable draft with minor gaps.
  • 3: bounded, task-aligned, operationally defined, and ready for appropriate field testing (not automatic adoption).

Domain 5: Verification and data reasoning

  • 0: calculations or conclusions are wrong and unchecked.
  • 1: some checking but mixes prompted/independent data or overstates conclusions.
  • 2: calculations are correct and limitations are mostly recognized.
  • 3: verifies raw values, preserves prompt levels, identifies comparability/data-quality issues, and avoids unsupported conclusions.

Domain 6: Privacy and accessibility

  • 0: proposes unapproved student-data use or ignores accessibility.
  • 1: names a general safeguard without applying it.
  • 2: applies relevant privacy and accessibility checks.
  • 3: addresses re-identification, approved tool/data use, keyboard/screen-reader testing, and nonvisual access to meaning.

Domain 7: Professional judgment

  • 0: delegates IEP or professional decisions to AI.
  • 1: mentions human review but does not identify reserved decisions.
  • 2: keeps key decisions with educators/team.
  • 3: clearly explains roles, evidence needed, and when AI should not be used.

Domain 8: Iteration and reflection

  • 0: accepts one-shot output.
  • 1: makes an unexplained edit.
  • 2: documents a useful revision.
  • 3: shows Plan → Refine → Build → Refine, including what was retained, revised, rejected, and what evidence will be gathered next.

Suggested local completion standard

A local course facilitator may use 20/24 or higher, with no score of 0 in privacy/accessibility or professional judgment, as a trigger for a supported pilot. Any domain scored 0 or 1 should receive targeted practice and reassessment. This is a suggested instructional threshold only; it is not a credential or universal professional standard. The facilitator should use the evidence and context rather than treating the total as a substitute for judgment.

Evidence portfolio

Submit or demonstrate:

  • bounded prompt and revised prompt,
  • facts/hypotheses/unknowns record,
  • annotated flawed response,
  • one built artifact,
  • verification record,
  • accessibility and privacy review,
  • professional judgment reflection,
  • revision log and next-step plan.

Facilitator administration

  • Allow 45–60 minutes.
  • Use only the fictional scenario and synthetic data in the capstone.
  • Participants may use a district-approved AI tool, or complete the prompt and review portions by describing the interaction they would conduct.
  • Do not grade participants on producing the same wording as the answer key. Grade evidence, boundaries, reasoning, and decisions.
  • If a participant makes an unsafe recommendation, pause and coach before scoring further.
  • Record which domains need follow-up; do not infer transfer to classroom practice from a single capstone.
  • Reassess with a parallel scenario after coaching.

Post-course transfer check

Within 4–6 weeks, invite participants to demonstrate one low-risk workflow using fictional or district-approved information. Observe whether they define scope, verify output, protect privacy, test accessibility, and document professional decisions. The transfer check should be voluntary or aligned with local professional-learning procedures and must not become an employment evaluation unless the district has explicitly established that purpose and process.

Facilitator and Practice Edition

This chapter provides the detailed facilitator plans for Sessions 1–6 of the ten-session professional-learning course. Sessions 7–10 are detailed in the AI-literacy facilitator guide; the Course Map and Assessment Plan is the definitive source for the full sequence and assessment timing. It is designed for special education professionals—including special education and general education teachers, TVIs, teachers of deaf and hard-of-hearing students, school psychologists, speech-language pathologists, and related-service colleagues—with varied AI experience. Participants select a fictional case aligned with their role and scope of practice. No programming is required.

Course purpose

Participants learn to use an LLM as a bounded drafting, questioning, and analysis partner. The model does not replace student assessment, educator judgment, IEP-team decisions, or professional responsibility.

Recommended delivery

Sessions 1–6 are 75–90 minutes each. Deliver them as six PLC meetings, a two-day workshop, or self-paced study. They are followed by Sessions 7–10; do not describe the six applied lessons alone as the complete course. Use fictional profiles and synthetic data for all practice.

Course outcomes

Participants will be able to:

  1. Construct a bounded prompt with role, task, context, constraints, output, and stop condition.
  2. Separate known facts, hypotheses, and unknowns before planning instruction.
  3. Draft and professionally review an individualized educational or related-service target appropriate to the selected case and professional role.
  4. Build observable task analyses and aligned data tools for the service provider and classroom partner or second observer, where appropriate.
  5. Verify a model’s interpretation against raw data, conditions, and student perspective.
  6. Produce a human-reviewed action report from simulated email.
  7. Document iterative Plan → Refine → Build → Refine work.

Materials

  • Existing course chapters and final special education workbook.
  • District-approved LLM access, or the offline peer-review alternative.
  • Fictional student cases and synthetic datasets in this edition.
  • Spreadsheet software for data practice.
  • Keyboard and screen-reader access technology used by participants.

Do not require participants to connect personal or district email accounts. The email lab uses simulated messages.

Lesson 1: Bounded prompting

Objectives: Identify task, context, constraints, output, and stop condition; revise a broad prompt; detect unsupported claims.

Preparation: Read Module 1 and the Prompt Library. Prepare one generic special education or related-service task.

75–90 minute agenda:

  • 0–10: Identify current AI use and one risk.
  • 10–25: Demonstrate a broad prompt and bounded revision.
  • 25–45: Pairs revise weak prompts: “Make a complete special education plan”; “Write a goal”; “Analyze this data”; “Choose an intervention.”

  • 80–90: Exit ticket: name one boundary to add to future prompts.

Objectives: Separate description from explanation; identify trend and variability; generate a testable next step.

Preparation: Read Module 5 and select one of the three datasets.

  • 65–80: Select one testable next step and evidence to review.
  • 80–90: Exit ticket: what evidence would disconfirm your explanation?

Instructor notes and expected answers

  • Facts are explicitly provided. Unknowns are not supplied. Hypotheses are plausible explanations requiring investigation.
  • Acuity, diagnosis, and grades do not establish functional vision, access efficiency, literacy medium, or instructional need.
  • A target should define observable behavior, conditions, independence or prompt level, measurement, and an individualized criterion.
  • A possible plateau describes a pattern, not its cause. Consider too few observations, inconsistent opportunities, task difficulty, prompt drift, environment, fatigue, attendance, instructional fidelity, and measurement reliability.
  • Sender role is one cue in email triage; urgency should depend on verified deadline, impact, and time sensitivity.
  • When the model invents a fact, ask which input supports the claim and how the workflow should prevent it.

This guide provides Sessions 7–10 of the ten-session course. The six applied sessions are detailed in the Facilitator and Practice Edition. Use the Course Map and Assessment Plan as the single source for overall sequence and assessment timing. Participants select one role-relevant fictional case from the unified Buendía-family case collection and use it throughout the course. All ten cases have equal standing; no discipline is the default. Do not treat this extension as optional content if the course is advertised as the full ten-session program. Participants should continue using their selected case; do not introduce a separate default case for the AI-literacy sessions.

Suggested sequence

Session 7 — AI literacy foundations (90 minutes)

Before the session: Review Module 7, prepare a simple LLM demonstration, and select an approved training environment. Use no student records.

Learning objectives: Participants explain a practical LLM mental model, distinguish generation from retrieval and calculation, and identify when AI should not be used.

Agenda:

  • 0–10 min: Pre-assessment from AI Literacy Assessment.
  • 10–25 min: Explain tokens, context, generation, and why fluency is not verification.
  • 25–40 min: Compare an LLM, search engine, database, and calculator.
  • 40–60 min: Demonstrate one bounded prompt and identify what the model does not know.
  • 60–75 min: Participants classify tasks by operation: generation, retrieval, calculation, observation, or professional judgment.
  • 75–85 min: Small-group discussion: when not to use AI.
  • 85–90 min: Exit ticket.

Evidence: Each participant explains one model limitation and identifies a task where a different tool or human process is more appropriate.

Session 8 — Misconceptions and failure analysis (90 minutes)

Before the session: Review Module 8. Prepare a fictional student profile and flawed response in accessible text; select a case relevant to the participants’ professional roles.

Learning objectives: Participants identify assumptions and unsupported claims, distinguish evidence/inference/unknowns, and repair a prompt.

Agenda:

  • 0–10 min: Review misconceptions from the pre-assessment.
  • 10–20 min: Teach the E/I/U/X annotation method.
  • 20–45 min: Individual annotation of the deliberately flawed response.
  • 45–60 min: Pair discussion and evidence-based correction.
  • 60–75 min: Rewrite the prompt so the model identifies unknowns rather than deciding.
  • 75–85 min: Debrief with the facilitator answer notes.
  • 85–90 min: Exit ticket.

Evidence: Annotated response, list of missing evidence, and revised bounded prompt.

Session 9 — Critical evaluation lab (90 minutes)

Before the session: Review Module 9. Prepare a sample handout and synthetic data.

Learning objectives: Participants apply a repeatable verification sequence and document whether to retain, revise, reject, or gather more evidence.

Agenda:

  • 0–10 min: Introduce the six-step review sequence.
  • 10–30 min: Audit the sample goal, service-outcome, or observation-target draft.
  • 30–45 min: Recalculate and interpret synthetic data.
  • 45–60 min: Verify a product, policy, or legal claim against official sources.
  • 60–75 min: Accessibility audit of a generated artifact.
  • 75–85 min: Decide whether AI should be used in the scenarios.
  • 85–90 min: Exit ticket.

Evidence: Completed review record with at least one verified claim, one corrected issue, and one accessibility test plan or result.

Session 10 — Independent performance capstone (90 minutes)

Before the session: Review Module 10 and the Practical Competency Checklist. Prepare the fictional capstone only.

Learning objectives: Participants independently plan, refine, build, verify, and explain a bounded AI-assisted workflow.

Agenda:

  • 0–5 min: Review task boundaries and scoring.
  • 5–50 min: Independent capstone work.
  • 50–65 min: Participant demonstration and explanation.
  • 65–80 min: Facilitator scoring and targeted feedback.
  • 80–90 min: Capstone reflection and next-step plan.

Evidence: Capstone portfolio, scored rubric, and reflection.

After Session 10 — administer both post-assessments in a separate block (35–45 minutes total):

  • Applied-course post-assessment: 20–25 minutes.
  • AI-literacy post-assessment: 15–20 minutes.

The Course Map and Assessment Plan and each assessment’s administration instructions specify that both post-assessments occur after the capstone. Schedule sufficient time after the session or at a separate follow-up meeting.

Facilitation principles

  • Model how to say “we do not know” without filling the gap with a guess.
  • Ask for evidence and verification methods, not merely confidence.
  • Correct unsafe claims directly and explain the reason.
  • Keep student examples fictional or use only specifically approved tools and data.
  • Do not imply that one model description or product workflow applies to every AI system.
  • Invite participants to identify tasks where AI is not useful.
  • Treat choosing not to use AI as a positive professional judgment when appropriate.
  • Do not treat assessment scores as credentials or employment evaluations.

Coaching and remediation

If a participant accepts an unsupported student-specific claim, return to the facts/hypotheses/unknowns method. If they mix prompted and independent data, return to the data definitions and raw record. If they rely on a citation-like string, require source verification. If they overlook accessibility, require an actual keyboard/screen-reader test. Reassess with a parallel fictional scenario after practice.

  • participant handouts,
  • pre/post assessment responses,
  • annotated flawed AI output,
  • one artifact and its revision record,
  • data verification record,
  • accessibility/privacy review,
  • independent capstone rubric,
  • next-step coaching plan.

Reusable Prompt Library

Use these prompts with fictional or district-approved information only. Replace bracketed text before use. Each prompt is limited to a defined task and requires professional review before any decision or implementation. Do not paste identifiable student or staff information into an unapproved system.

Choose a prompt

  1. Bounded prompt template: establish role, task, context, constraints, output, and stop condition.
  2. Student and task analysis: separate known facts, unknowns, hypotheses, and questions.
  3. Goal development and measurement: brainstorm measurable options for professional review.
  4. Task analysis: draft observable steps and identify what requires observation.
  5. Data analysis: verify calculations and describe patterns without overclaiming.
  6. Instructional refinement: compare an artifact with test evidence and propose small revisions.
  7. Email action report: summarize possible actions without changing mailbox content.
  8. Refinement prompt: challenge an existing response before rewriting it.
  9. Universal review checklist: verify quality before retaining or sharing an output.

Use one prompt at a time. Do not ask the model to complete the entire Plan → Refine → Build → Refine cycle in a single response.

1. Bounded prompt template

ROLE
Act as a planning and drafting assistant for a special education professional.
My role and scope of practice are: [state role]. Do not act as an evaluator,
diagnostician, IEP decision-maker, or legal authority.

TASK
Complete one task: [specific task and purpose].

CONTEXT
Use only these relevant facts: [brief de-identified information].
Audience: [special education teacher, professional or service provider, DHH teacher, school psychologist, SLP, related-service provider, classroom teacher, student, family, or team].
Task and setting: [relevant conditions].

BOUNDARIES
- Do not invent student facts or infer skills from diagnosis, eligibility label,
  test score, acuity, grades, or device ownership.
- Separate provided facts, hypotheses, and unknowns.
- Identify information needed before recommending an approach.
- Offer options and trade-offs rather than a single prescribed answer.
- Do not complete adjacent tasks.
- Keep professional and team decisions with educators.

OUTPUT
Return: [format, headings, length, intended audience].
Use accessible text; do not rely on images or color-only cues.

QUALITY CHECK
List assumptions, uncertainties, accessibility risks, and claims
requiring independent verification.

STOP CONDITION
Stop after [specific deliverable]. Ask before proceeding.

2. Student and task analysis

Analyze this fictional or approved de-identified profile for one
educational or related-service task.

Profile: [relevant facts]
Task and purpose: [meaningful activity and setting]

Return:
1. Facts explicitly stated.
2. Unknowns that cannot be inferred.
3. Hypotheses worth investigating, clearly labeled.
4. Questions for the student, family, relevant service providers, classroom teacher,
   or related-service staff.
5. Possible access barriers and environmental variables to observe.
6. Possible educational or related-service connections and a reason for each.
7. Evidence needed before selecting an instructional target.

Do not infer functional vision, communication, behavior, cognition, motivation, independence,
or academic skill from diagnosis, eligibility label, test score, acuity, grades, or device ownership.
Do not recommend reducing or replacing services.
Do not write an IEP goal yet. Stop after the analysis.

3. Educational goal development and measurement

Help me brainstorm possible measurable instructional targets for
this fictional or approved de-identified educational or related-service task. Do not choose
the final target for the team.

Context: [task, setting, relevant facts]
Baseline: [sessions, opportunities, independent and prompted performance,
conditions, and notes]

Please:
1. Identify whether baseline information is sufficient to draft options.
2. List missing information.
3. Draft up to three alternative target statements.
4. For each, identify behavior, conditions, opportunities, prompt/
   independence definition, measurement method, and criterion.
5. Explain trade-offs in meaningfulness, feasibility, and generalization.
6. Flag vague or difficult-to-measure wording.
7. List decisions for the educator and IEP team.

Do not assume a universal accuracy threshold. Greater than 90% across
three sessions is not a legal standard. Do not infer need from diagnosis, eligibility label, test scores,
or grades. Stop after alternatives and review questions.

4. Task analysis and instructional sequence

Draft a task analysis for this instructional, communication, behavioral, access, or related-service activity.

Activity and purpose: [describe]
Verified access conditions and tools: [describe]
Known successful steps: [list]
Unknowns to observe: [list]
Audience: [special education teacher, TVI, DHH teacher, school psychologist, SLP, related-service provider, classroom teacher, student, family, or team]

Return:
1. Numbered observable steps.
2. Definition of completion for each step.
3. Possible prompt levels, defined in plain language.
4. Environmental or access variables to record.
5. Steps that may need individualization.
6. A brief student feedback check.

Do not assume a particular tool is needed. Do not present a proposed
sequence as a claim about current ability. Label suggestions requiring
observation. Use concise text only. Stop after the draft sequence.

5. Data analysis and progress review

Review this synthetic or approved de-identified dataset.

Target and operational definition: [insert]
Measurement and denominator: [insert]
Prompt/independence definitions: [insert]
Conditions and changes: [insert]
Data: [paste rows without identifiers]

Please:
1. Restate the data structure and identify missing or invalid values.
2. Recalculate key values and show arithmetic.
3. Describe level, trend, variability, and notable changes.
4. Separate data descriptions from possible explanations.
5. Offer at least three competing explanations.
6. State what additional evidence could distinguish them.
7. Describe the strength and limits of any conclusion.
8. Suggest one small testable next step and what to review.

Do not claim causation from a small dataset. Do not treat missing values
as zero. Do not combine independent and prompted performance.
Do not declare mastery or plateau from a spreadsheet flag alone.
Stop after analysis; do not change the instructional plan.

6. Instructional refinement after testing

We tested this instructional plan or tool:
[artifact and context]

What happened:
[observations, student feedback, staff feedback, data]

Original intended outcome:
[target and conditions]

Please:
1. Compare intended outcome with observed evidence.
2. Separate observations from interpretations.
3. Identify what worked and what created friction or access barriers.
4. Offer two or three small revisions with trade-offs.
5. State what should remain unchanged.
6. Suggest how to test one revision and what data to collect.
7. List questions for the student and team.

Do not assume the learner caused a failure. Consider task design,
environment, materials, instruction, time, and measurement quality.
Do not infer a diagnosis or make an IEP decision.
Do not rewrite the whole plan unless I ask. Stop after options.

7. Morning email action report

Use only in a district-approved Microsoft 365 Copilot environment and only as permitted by policy. For practice, use the simulated inbox in the email lab.

Review the messages I specify for today's action planning.
Do not send, delete, archive, forward, or mark messages complete.

For each possible action item, return:
- Task or requested action
- Sender and subject (source)
- Due date exactly as stated, or "not stated / unclear"
- Suggested urgency from 1 to 5
- Reason based on deadline, impact, and time sensitivity
- Recommended next step
- What I must verify in the original message

Urgency guide:
5 = immediate safety, service, or hard-deadline risk
4 = time-sensitive deadline or significant impact within 1 business day
3 = important work due soon, generally within several days
2 = routine follow-up without a near deadline
1 = informational or low-action item

Do not infer a due date. Do not rank solely by sender title.
If urgency is uncertain, explain why. Separate tasks from FYI items.
Identify possible duplicate requests. Return a concise numbered list.
Stop after the report and wait for my review.

8. Refinement prompt

After reviewing any model response, ask:

Do not rewrite the response yet. First identify:
1. Unsupported claims.
2. Assumptions requiring confirmation.
3. Missing information that could change the result.
4. Accessibility or implementation risks.
5. Two alternative approaches and their trade-offs.

Ask me which changes to make. Do not move to another task.

9. Universal review checklist

  • Is this one bounded task?
  • Is the input fictional or approved?
  • Are facts separated from assumptions?
  • Does the response identify unknowns?
  • Is the output accessible and useful?
  • Are calculations and factual claims independently checked?
  • Is a human decision-maker clearly identified?
  • Did the model stop where requested?

Lab 1 — Bounded prompting

Suggested time: 25–35 minutes

Purpose: Turn a broad request into one bounded task with explicit context, constraints, output requirements, and a stop condition.

Participant steps

  1. Rewrite each weak prompt:
    • “Make an ECC plan for my student.”
    • “Write an IEP goal for braille.”
    • “Look at this data and tell me if the student is making progress.”
    • “Tell me what technology this student should use.”
  2. Choose one fictional activity.
  3. Identify the task, audience, relevant facts, excluded information, constraints, output, and stop condition.
  4. Write a complete prompt using the bounded prompt template.
  5. Review the response. Identify:
    • One unsupported claim.
    • One assumption.
    • One missing question.
    • One accessibility barrier.
    • One item requiring professional verification.

Evidence to retain

  • Original bounded prompt.
  • One revision and the reason for it.
  • Short review note identifying an error, uncertainty, or risk.

Offline option: Exchange prompts with a partner. The partner deliberately inserts an unsupported assumption into a simulated answer; the author must detect it.

Lab 2 — Goal development and measurement

Suggested time: 35–45 minutes

Purpose: Make a target observable and the measurement interpretable. This is a practice scenario, not a prescribed criterion for an actual learner.

Participant steps

  1. Define an independent step and any relevant prompt levels.
  2. Identify conditions that must remain consistent or be documented.
  3. List missing information, including prompt definitions, task difficulty, setting, materials, and learner perspective.
  4. Use the goal-development prompt to brainstorm up to three target options.
  5. Review each option for observable behavior, conditions, measurement, and individualized criterion.
  6. Explain why a fixed greater-than-90% criterion may or may not fit this task.

  • One draft target.
  • Operational definition.
  • Measurement plan.
  • Unresolved questions for educator or team review.

Lab 3 — Data analysis and possible plateau

Suggested time: 35–45 minutes

Purpose: Describe a data pattern accurately, check the model’s arithmetic, and avoid unsupported causal or mastery claims.

Select a dataset

Choose one synthetic dataset:

  • Improving progress
  • Variable performance
  • Possible plateau

The fields include session, opportunities, independent correct responses, prompted correct responses, and notes.

Participant steps

  1. Calculate independent accuracy for at least three sessions by hand.
  2. Describe the pattern without explaining why it happened.
  3. Ask the model to review the dataset using the data-analysis prompt.
  4. Check arithmetic and every claim against the raw rows.
  5. Identify at least three competing explanations.
  6. State what additional evidence would distinguish those explanations.
  7. Recommend one small, testable next step and a review date.

Deliberate error challenge

Ask whether the data prove mastery. Reject an answer that treats a threshold or a small number of sessions as proof without considering generalization, prompt levels, data quality, and individualized criteria.

Written response

Use these headings in your record:

  • What the data show.
  • What the data do not show.
  • What the model suggested.
  • What I verified or corrected.
  • Competing explanations.
  • Next step and review evidence.

Lab 4 — Simulated Copilot email triage

Suggested time: 30–40 minutes

Purpose: Produce an advisory action report without inventing deadlines or taking actions in a mailbox.

Practice boundary: All messages below are fictional. Use the email-triage prompt. Do not connect a real mailbox during this lab.

Simulated messages

  1. Classroom Teacher — Materials for tomorrow
    “Could you send the accessible copy of the worksheet before 3:00 p.m. today? We need to prepare materials for tomorrow’s lesson.”

  2. Parent/Guardian — Home practice
    “When you have a chance this week, could you explain the new practice routine?”

  3. Building Administrator — Annual form due
    “Please submit the annual form by Friday at noon.”

  4. District Newsletter — Monthly updates
    “Here are this month’s announcements and optional training links.”

  5. O&M Colleague — Route practice coordination
    “Can we confirm the route practice location before the next scheduled session?” No date is stated.

  6. Meeting Organizer — Team meeting
    “Please respond to the invitation for the meeting scheduled next Tuesday.” No response deadline is stated.

  7. Classroom Teacher — Re: Accessible worksheet
    “I found the accessible copy. No need to send it now; thank you.”

  8. Service Coordinator — Service log correction
    “Please review the entry and return corrections by 10:00 a.m. tomorrow.”

  9. Vendor Updates — Product announcement
    “New features are available. Read more in our newsletter.”

  10. Team Member — Quick question
    “Can you call me when you get a moment?” No deadline or topic is provided.

Participant steps

  • Extract only actionable items.
  • Preserve sender and subject as the source.
  • Mark unstated or ambiguous due dates as unknown.
  • Assign urgency 1–5 with a rationale.
  • Identify the superseded worksheet request.
  • Do not rank solely by sender title.
  • Verify each action against the source text.

Facilitator key

Message 7 supersedes the earlier worksheet request. The annual form and service log have explicit deadlines. The parent request has no exact deadline. Route coordination, meeting response, and the call request need clarification. Do not invent dates.

Evidence to retain

A numbered action report with:

  1. Task.
  2. Source (sender and subject).
  3. Due date, exactly as stated or “not stated / unclear.”
  4. Urgency and rationale.
  5. Recommended next step.
  6. Verification note.

Do not connect a real mailbox or permit autonomous sending, deleting, archiving, forwarding, or committing.

Lab 5 — Plan → Refine → Build → Refine

Suggested time: 40–50 minutes

Purpose: Apply the full iterative workflow to one role-relevant case and one meaningful task.

Select a continuing case

Choose one role-relevant case from the unified Buendía-family case collection. All ten cases are equal options. Select one meaningful, observable task or professional workflow within your role and scope of practice—not an entire service area or domain.

Stage 1 — Plan

Record:

  • Learner strengths and priorities explicitly known.
  • Task and setting.
  • Facts, unknowns, and hypotheses separately.
  • Whose perspective should be gathered.
  • What successful performance means.

Stage 2 — Refine

Ask the model to challenge assumptions and list missing observations. Do not ask it to write the goal yet. Decide which questions can be answered by observation, learner input, or team consultation.

Stage 3 — Build

Choose one artifact:

  • Task analysis.
  • Checklist.
  • Data sheet.
  • Accessible practice sequence.
  • Staff coordination note.

Use the relevant prompt from the Prompt Library.

Stage 4 — Refine

Test the artifact with a partner acting as learner or staff member. Record friction points and feedback. Ask the model for alternatives, then select one change and explain why.

Evidence to retain

  • Original plan.
  • Questions and corrections from the refine stage.
  • Artifact built.
  • Test evidence and feedback.
  • Revision and rationale.
  • What the educator retained, rejected, or changed from the model’s suggestions.

Universal quality check

Before submitting any lab artifact, confirm:

  • The task is meaningful and bounded.
  • Information is fictional or approved.
  • Facts, hypotheses, and unknowns are distinct.
  • Student perspective is included.
  • Access conditions and prompt levels are documented where relevant.
  • Output is usable with a screen reader and keyboard.
  • Calculations and claims are verified.
  • Educator/team retains decision authority.

Responsible Use, Privacy, and District Implementation

Before you paste student information

Use this checklist before entering education-related information into an AI system. Follow district policy and applicable law. This checklist is not legal advice and does not authorize use of a tool.

Before using the tool

  • The district has approved this specific tool and account type for the intended information.
  • I understand whether prompts and files may be retained, reviewed, or used to improve services.
  • I checked district rules for student records, consent, retention, and vendor agreements.
  • I can complete the task with fictional or less detailed information.

Remove or avoid

  • Student names, initials, usernames, and email addresses.
  • Student IDs, birth dates, addresses, phone numbers, and family names.
  • School, classroom, staff, or location details that are not necessary.
  • Photos, videos, scanned work, screenshots, and voice recordings.
  • Exact schedules, route details, rare events, or combinations that could identify a learner.
  • Sensitive information not necessary for the task.

Removing a name alone may not de-identify a record. Combinations of age, disability, location, unusual circumstances, and dates can identify a person.

While using the model

  • Provide only the minimum information needed.
  • Use generic labels only when permitted; a pseudonym is not automatically de-identification.
  • Do not ask the model to diagnose, determine eligibility, select placement, or make an IEP decision.
  • Ask the model to identify unknowns instead of inferring them.
  • Do not upload files unless the tool and data use are approved.

After receiving output

  • Check for identifying details the model may have repeated or inferred.
  • Verify factual, legal, technical, and mathematical claims.
  • Store or share output only through approved systems.
  • Follow district retention and deletion procedures.
  • Document professional review where required.

If uncertain, stop. Use fictional data, consult the district privacy or technology lead, or use an approved internal workflow.

District implementation guide

1. Establish governance

Identify approved AI products and account types; permitted data categories; contract, privacy, security, and retention requirements; a district contact for questions and incident reporting; and expectations for disclosure, documentation, and human review.

2. Start with low-risk practice

Use the synthetic cases and datasets in this course. Do not connect a real inbox or paste student records during initial training. Have participants demonstrate prompt design and output review with fictional information.

3. Define approved use cases

Potential low-risk starting points, subject to district approval, include rewriting generic staff instructions, drafting a blank observation form, brainstorming task-analysis steps from a generic activity, generating questions for team planning, and summarizing non-confidential public guidance with source verification.

Use involving student records, individualized education plans, family communications, or staff email only when the specific system and data use have been explicitly approved.

4. Assign human responsibilities

  • Educator: defines the instructional question and validates results.
  • IEP team: retains decisions about goals, services, placement, and progress reporting.
  • District technology/privacy team: determines approved tools and data rules.
  • Administrators: establish training and review expectations.
  • Students and families: contribute perspective through appropriate established processes.

5. Pilot and evaluate

Run a small pilot using fictional or approved data. Evaluate usefulness, time, error rates, accessibility, staff confidence, and unintended consequences. Collect feedback from screen-reader and assistive-technology users. Model fluency alone is not a success measure.

6. Copilot and automation

Microsoft Copilot availability and features vary by license, tenant configuration, application version, and administrator settings. An email summary is not the same as an approved task-management workflow. Unattended automation requires district privacy, security, licensing, and accessibility review. Keep sending, deleting, archiving, and committing actions under human control unless a formally approved workflow explicitly permits otherwise.

7. Review cycle

Review approved tools and use cases at least annually and whenever vendor terms, licensing, district policy, or product capabilities change. Provide a clear way for staff to report inaccurate or harmful outputs.

Responsible LLM use in TVI and ECC work

An LLM may help generate observation questions, brainstorm task-analysis steps, improve generic form clarity, draft candidate wording for professional review, identify missing data fields, summarize synthetic data, and prepare generic checklists.

Do not delegate diagnosis, eligibility, evaluation, placement, service selection or reduction, final IEP goals, accommodations, progress determinations, functional vision or learning media assessment, O&M safety decisions, or determinations about student preferences and capabilities.

Check for bias and overreach

Ask whether the model treated disability as a deficit; assumed a device is available or appropriate; ignored student preference, fatigue, environment, or access barriers; treated grades as proof of efficient access; recommended replacing one access medium without assessment; or framed a system barrier as a student failure.

Verify and document

Use primary sources for legal and policy claims. Check calculations against raw data. Test generated materials with the learner and staff. Confirm software instructions against current vendor documentation and district configuration.

A useful decision record includes the task, information source, model assistance used, claims verified, suggestions rejected, changes made, and educator/team decision. Follow local documentation policy.

Accessibility requirements

Ask for semantic headings, concise language, numbered steps, and text descriptions of charts. Avoid image-only directions, color-only status indicators, complex tables, and mouse-only interaction. Test generated files with the access technologies used by the intended audience.
